\begin{abstract}
This paper compares local Physics-Informed Neural Cellular Automata (PI-NCA) with
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and global neural operators such as the
Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) for learning the dynamics of partial differential
equations (PDEs). To ensure that comparisons rest on verified implementations, we
migrate a PyTorch PI-NCA codebase to JAX/Flax behind a correctness gate in which every
ported solver and model is asserted equal to a verbatim PyTorch reference to tolerance
before any experiment is conducted, and then evaluate all architectures on a suite of
eight 2-D and six 3-D PDEs under one shared autoregressive-emulation protocol. The
architectures include a conservative flux-divergence PI-NCA (\texttt{DeepFluxNCA}), an
unconstrained NCA, four hybrid designs (\texttt{MultiScaleFluxNCA},
\texttt{BoundedConsFluxNCA}, \texttt{SpectralFluxNCA}, \texttt{MultiChannelFluxNCA}),
an FNO, and continuous PINN and DeepONet baselines.

We find that no single architecture attains the lowest error across all PDEs; the
prevailing inductive bias is instead determined by the PDE's structure---whether the
quantity is conserved, whether the field is bounded, how far information propagates per
step, and how much fine-scale content the solution carries. On smooth local diffusion
(heat), the multi-scale conservative NCA attains the lowest rel-L2 ($0.0064$--$0.018$)
at approximately $200\times$ better parameter efficiency than the FNO; the FNO attains
the lowest error on fine-scale, globally coupled, and non-conservative regimes
(Allen--Cahn $0.0068$, Navier--Stokes $0.098$); the unconstrained NCA attains the
lowest error on non-conservative reaction systems (FitzHugh--Nagumo $0.125$); and all
simple emulators diverge on the stiff fourth-order Cahn--Hilliard equation until an
explicit output-bounding step is added, a step that conflicts with exact conservation
and is reconciled by a bounded conservation-preserving update. A spectral diagnostic
quantifies the trade-off: the NCAs concentrate their residual error in fine scales
(high-frequency error fraction $0.44$--$0.89$) whereas the FNO's error is
broadband-flat ($0.004$), which accounts for the FNO's advantage on sharp-interface
problems despite the NCA's lower total error on smooth ones. The 2-D regime ordering
reproduces in full 3-D, indicating that the dependence of architecture performance on
PDE structure is dimension-independent. All results are reported under a single fixed
seed with a fixed pre-seeding protocol; the reduced-scale CPU setting is stated
explicitly; and all 56 of 56 correctness checks pass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Neural emulators for partial differential equations constitute a densely populated
research area. On one side sit \emph{Physics-Informed Neural Networks} (PINNs), which
fit a continuous function $u_\phi(x,t)$ to a single initial-value problem (IVP) by
minimizing the PDE residual via automatic differentiation. On another sit
\emph{neural operators} such as the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) and DeepONet, which
learn a map between function spaces and generalize across initial conditions. On a
third, less explored side sit \emph{Neural Cellular Automata} (NCAs): grids of
identical local cells that update by a learned rule, analogous to a finite-difference
stencil with trainable weights.

This work does not present NCAs as a universally superior method. The objective is to
characterize which method prevails in which regime, and to document where NCAs
underperform and why. An NCA is a local model: information propagates at most one cell
per step. A PINN is continuous but solves a single IVP at a time. An FNO is global
within one layer but incurs a cost in parameters and in the smoothing induced by
spectral truncation. These are not competing implementations of the same idea; they
encode different inductive biases. The question addressed here is whether a PDE's
structure predicts which bias prevails.

\paragraph{PDE Structure and Inductive Bias.}
The behavior of a numerical emulator depends critically on how well its inductive
bias aligns with the governing PDE. Certain PDEs are \emph{local and conservative}:
heat diffuses smoothly and the total quantity is preserved. For such problems, a
local cellular automaton with built-in mass conservation is a near-ideal
match---it performs precisely the bookkeeping demanded by the physics at low
computational cost. Other PDEs are \emph{globally coupled}: in incompressible flow,
the pressure at any point depends instantaneously on the entire velocity field through
a Poisson equation. A local machine cannot propagate information rapidly enough; a
global spectral operator such as the FNO is the appropriate choice. Still other PDEs
contain \emph{source terms} that create or destroy the conserved quantity (e.g.,
reaction terms), rendering a conservation-enforcing prior actively detrimental. Finally,
some PDEs are \emph{stiff and bounded} (such as the 4th-order Cahn--Hilliard equation,
whose order parameter is confined to $[-1,1]$), where the primary risk is not locality
but numerical blow-up, and the remedy is output bounding. From this perspective, the
relevant question is not which architecture is best in the abstract, but which
inductive bias best matches the PDE's structural characteristics.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions, each grounded
in the project's own multi-seed and single-seed benchmark results:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{correctness-gated JAX migration} of a PyTorch PI-NCA codebase, in
        which every ported solver and model is asserted equal to a verbatim reference
        before any architecture work begins (56/56 tests; an NHWC-axis Laplacian bug and
        a Gray--Scott $dt=2.0$ instability were identified in the process).
  \item A \textbf{regime map} over ten 2-D phenomena and six 3-D phenomena, conducted
        under one shared autoregressive-emulation protocol, showing that no single
        architecture attains the lowest error across all PDEs and that the PDE's
        structure determines which architecture prevails.
  \item Four \textbf{hybrid architectures} that compose the corresponding inductive
        biases and attain the lowest error in their target regime: a multi-scale
        conservative NCA on smooth local transport (more accurate than the FNO at
        $\sim$1\% of its parameters) and a bounded conservation-preserving NCA on stiff
        Cahn--Hilliard (the only tested model that is both accurate and mass-conserving).
  \item A \textbf{six-ablation study} (A1--A6) that isolates which component drives
        performance in each regime: conservation, output bounding, perception size,
        multi-step training, spectral mixing, and parameter count.
  \item A \textbf{spectral error diagnostic} that accounts for the NCA-versus-FNO
        trade-off in terms of where each model concentrates its error in frequency space.
  \item A \textbf{resolution-transfer study} across training and evaluation grids
        ($16^2$ to $48^2$), showing that transfer is regime-dependent: the FNO is
        resolution-invariant on Navier--Stokes but not in the autoregressive
        local-diffusion setting.
  \item Evidence that the \textbf{regime ordering is dimension-independent}, reproducing
        in full 3-D.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Experimental Scope.} All experiments are conducted at \emph{reduced scale}
on a single CPU (no GPU was available). Absolute error values therefore do not reflect
GPU-scale performance; the \emph{relative ordering} of architectures is the primary
object of study, and this ordering is expected to transfer when identical configurations
are re-run at full scale. Results use a single fixed seed (42) under the pre-seeding protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), abbreviated ``better-start'' in table captions, consistent
with the original implementations. The associated variance trade-off is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Neural Cellular Automata.} NCAs maintain a state grid and apply an
identical learned local rule to every cell, perceiving the neighborhood with fixed or
learned depthwise convolutions and updating through a pointwise MLP. The canonical
differentiable NCA is \emph{Growing Neural Cellular Automata}
\cite{mordvintsev2020growing}; the classical link between cellular automata and PDEs
predates it \cite{omohundro1003}. Subsequent work extended NCAs to textures and
continuous space-time dynamics (NoiseNCA \cite{noisenca}, $\mu$NCA
\cite{munca}, DyNCA \cite{dynca}), to geometric robustness (isotropic
\cite{isonca}, steerable \cite{steerablenca}), and surveyed the frontier
\cite{cellstopixels,universalnca,neuralparticle,difflogic}. For hardware
acceleration, CAX \cite{cax} provides a JAX library of CA primitives, evaluated
directly in Section~\ref{sec:cax}.

\paragraph{Physics-Informed Neural Networks.} PINNs \cite{raissi2019pinn1,
raissi2019pinn2} train a network $u_\phi(x,t)$ to satisfy the PDE residual through
autodiff, with no labelled interior data. Their strengths (mesh-free, continuous,
data-free) and failure modes (spectral bias, loss-balancing, causality) are well
documented \cite{pinnreview,adaptivepinn,pinnlimits,autobalance,causalpinn,
fourierfeatures,spectralbias,preconditioning,pinnerror}.

\paragraph{Neural operators.} The FNO \cite{li2020fno} learns a map between function
spaces via spectral convolution, achieving a global receptive field in one layer;
universality and generalization analyses followed \cite{neuraloperator,fno_universal,
fno_rademacher}, as did a physics-informed variant (PINO \cite{pino}). DeepONet
\cite{lu2019deeponet} factorizes the operator into a branch (input function) and trunk
(query location) network \cite{pideeponet,resdeeponet,statoperator}. Graph neural
operators \cite{gno,egno} target irregular meshes; this work employs regular periodic
grids, so they are noted only for completeness.

\paragraph{Differentiable solvers and JAX SciML.} End-to-end gradients through a
numerical solver enable hybrid learning and inverse design \cite{torax,implicitneural,
diffsolverrom}; the heat solver used here is precisely a differentiable solver serving
as a teacher. The autoregressive-emulator setting follows the formalization of
APEBench/Exponax \cite{apebench}. The JAX ecosystem (jit/vmap/scan/sharding, Flax,
Optax) underpins the migration.

\paragraph{Positioning.} Prior work generally evaluates a single method family. This
study runs all families through one protocol and examines the regime question,
treating the hybrid designs as hypotheses to be tested rather than assumed improvements.

\section{Problem Setting and PDE Suite}
\label{sec:problem}
% ============================================================

We study time-dependent PDEs of the form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \mathcal{N}[u;\,\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}],
  \quad x \in \Omega,\; t\in[0,T], \qquad
  \mathcal{B}[u]=0 \text{ on } \partial\Omega,\quad u(x,0)=u_0(x),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}$ is a (possibly nonlinear) spatial operator and the domain is a
periodic grid. Two \emph{learning problems} must not be conflated:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Solving one IVP} (the PINN's native setting): fit $u(x,t)$ for a
        \emph{single} $u_0,\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}$.
  \item \textbf{Learning the solution operator} (the operator/NCA-emulator setting):
        learn a one-step map $u(t)\mapsto u(t+\Delta t)$ that \emph{generalizes across}
        initial conditions.
\end{itemize}
The NCA learns a \emph{local one-step update} trained to imitate a numerical solver ---
it is an \emph{autoregressive neural emulator}. PINNs and emulators are therefore not
measured on the same axis unless the protocol is designed carefully; they are treated as
separate paradigms (Section~\ref{sec:operators}).

\subsection{The equations}

\paragraph{Heat / diffusion (smooth, local, conservative).}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \alpha \nabla^2 u .
\end{equation}
The archetypal local, conservative parabolic PDE: total heat is conserved, and the
update is a discrete Laplacian---a natural match for a local stencil.

\paragraph{Advection--diffusion (linear transport).}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = -\,\mathbf{c}\cdot\nabla u + \alpha\nabla^2 u .
\end{equation}
Smooth but with a global drift; conservation is secondary to transport accuracy.

\paragraph{Allen--Cahn (non-conservative phase separation).}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \varepsilon^2 \nabla^2 u - W'(u), \qquad W'(u)=u^3-u,
\end{equation}
which develops \emph{sharp interfaces}---fine-scale content that penalizes spectral
smoothing.

\paragraph{Wave (2nd-order hyperbolic).}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{tt} u = c^2 \nabla^2 u,
\end{equation}
written as a first-order system in $(u,\partial_t u)$ for emulation.

\paragraph{Shallow-water (conservative, multi-field).} Hyperbolic conservation laws in
$(h, hu, hv)$ on a periodic domain, where both mass and momentum are conserved---the
multi-channel conservative regime.

\paragraph{Nagumo / FitzHugh--Nagumo (non-conservative reaction).} The bistable Nagumo
reaction--diffusion,
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = D\nabla^2 u + u(1-u)(u-a),
\end{equation}
and the two-field excitable FitzHugh--Nagumo activator--inhibitor system. The reaction
\emph{source term} modifies total mass, so conservation is the \emph{wrong} prior.

\paragraph{Cahn--Hilliard (stiff, 4th-order, bounded).}
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u = \nabla^2\!\left(-\varepsilon^2\nabla^2 u + W'(u)\right),
\end{equation}
a 4th-order, stiff, mass-conserving PDE whose order parameter is bounded in $[-1,1]$.
This is the regime where na{\"i}ve emulators diverge.

\paragraph{Gray--Scott (reaction--diffusion patterns).}
\begin{align}
  \partial_t u &= D_u\nabla^2 u - uv^2 + F(1-u),\\
  \partial_t v &= D_v\nabla^2 v + uv^2 - (F+k)v,
\end{align}
a challenging pattern-forming system (the stable $dt=1.0$ teacher is used; the
verbatim $dt=2.0$ diverges, as recorded during migration).

\subsection{Three-dimensional suite}
Six of these (heat, advection--diffusion, Allen--Cahn, Nagumo, Gray--Scott,
FitzHugh--Nagumo) are re-implemented in full 3-D on a $16^3$ grid in NDHWC layout, with
a 7-point Laplacian, a 3-D flux divergence over all three axes, and a 3-D spectral
convolution via \texttt{rfftn} (Section~\ref{sec:threed}).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ============================================================

\subsection{JAX migration and the correctness gate}
\label{sec:gate}

Before any new experiments, the entire PyTorch codebase (solvers and models) was ported
to JAX/Flax and \emph{asserted equal to verbatim PyTorch references to tolerance}. This
correctness gate is the foundation of every subsequent claim: if the JAX heat solver
does not match the PyTorch reference, no benchmark built on it can be trusted. The gate
identified two genuine bugs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{NHWC-axis bug} in the heat Laplacian (an isotropy violation),
        corrected and re-asserted;
  \item a \textbf{Gray--Scott $dt=2.0$ instability} in the verbatim parameters, which
        diverges; the benchmarks use the stable $dt=1.0$ teacher.
\end{itemize}
The gate stands at \textbf{56/56} tests (36 for the 2-D migration/suite/metrics, 11 for
the 3-D correctness suite, plus additional smoke/training checks), and must pass green
before any benchmark is trusted. Determinism flows entirely from explicit
\texttt{jax.random.PRNGKey(seed)} values; correctness is asserted to tolerances rather
than bit-equality because XLA CPU reductions are not bitwise-deterministic across
thread counts.

\subsection{Architectures and their update equations}
\label{sec:arch}

All neural emulators are used as \emph{one-step autoregressive maps}
$g_\theta:\, \mathrm{state}_t \mapsto \mathrm{state}_{t+1}$, trained by distillation
against a differentiable solver teacher and rolled out with \texttt{jax.lax.scan}. The
PINN is the exception (continuous, single-IVP). All update heads are zero-initialized,
so every model \emph{starts as the identity map}---a warm start.

\paragraph{(A) Plain NCA.} Perceive with a learnable $3\times3$ circular convolution
$P(\cdot)$, then a per-cell MLP (a stack of $1\times1$ convolutions) produces a residual
increment:
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1} = s_t + \mathrm{MLP}\!\big(\mathrm{ReLU}(P(s_t))\big).
\end{equation}
The residual ``$+s_t$'' makes the rule an explicit Euler step of a learned dynamical
system. There is \emph{no} conservation constraint. Cost is $O(N C F)$ per step
(linear in the number of cells $N$ for hidden width $F$). \textbf{Strength:} locality
and translation-equivariance match local PDE stencils, and computational cost is low.
\textbf{Weakness:} the absence of conservation leads to mass/energy drift, and locality
results in slow global-information propagation and long-horizon error accumulation.

\paragraph{(B) Conservative PI-NCA (DeepFluxNCA).} Same perceive+MLP backbone, but the
head predicts a \emph{flux field} $(f_x,f_y)$ and the update is its discrete
divergence:
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1} = s_t + \big(\mathrm{roll}(f_x)-f_x\big) + \big(\mathrm{roll}(f_y)-f_y\big).
  \label{eq:flux}
\end{equation}
Summed over a periodic grid, the increment \emph{telescopes to zero}, so mass is
conserved \emph{by construction} (exactly a finite-volume flux form).
Empirically the heat conservation error is $\sim$$10^{-4}$ versus $\sim$$10^{1}$ for an
unconstrained model. \textbf{Strength:} exact discrete conservation and stable
long-horizon mass behavior at the same low-cost locality. \textbf{Weakness:} the update
remains local, and it conserves mass but not necessarily at the correct rate---it can
conserve the wrong dynamics.

\paragraph{(C) MultiScaleFluxNCA (the heat-regime hybrid).} Perceive at three dilations
($d=1,2,4$), concatenate, then apply the conservative flux head of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} with an optional bounding step:
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1} = \mathcal{C}\Big[\,s_t + \mathrm{div}\big(\mathrm{flux}(\,
  [\,P_{d=1}(s_t)\,\|\,P_{d=2}(s_t)\,\|\,P_{d=4}(s_t)\,]\,)\big)\Big],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is identity when \texttt{bounds=None} and a clip-plus-mass-reproject
when bounds are set. Dilations $1/2/4$ widen the receptive field to $\pm4$ cells per
step (versus $\pm1$ for a single $3\times3$), reducing the locality gap at low
parameter cost, which accounts for its lower error than the FNO on heat at $\sim$1\%
of the parameters.

\paragraph{(D) BoundedConsFluxNCA (the Cahn--Hilliard-regime hybrid).} Records the total
mass, applies the conservative flux update, \emph{clips} the state to its physical range
$[-1,1]$ (stabilizing it but breaking conservation), then \emph{re-projects} to restore
the recorded total mass:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde s = \mathrm{clip}\big(s_t + \mathrm{div}(\mathrm{flux}(\dots)),\,-1,1\big),
  \qquad
  s_{t+1} = \mathrm{conserve\_energy}\big(\tilde s,\ \textstyle\sum s_t\big).
\end{equation}
This resolves the stability$\leftrightarrow$conservation tension identified in ablation
A1 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}): clipping alone corrects blow-up but destroys
conservation; the mass re-projection restores it, yielding a model that is bounded
\emph{and} conserving.

\paragraph{(E) SpectralFluxNCA (two-stream local+global).} A local conservative stream
(perceive $\to$ flux $\to$ divergence, conserving mass) is added to a global FNO-style
spectral stream (lift $\to$ \texttt{[SpectralConv2d + $1\times1$]}$\times$depth $\to$
project, zero-initialized):
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1} = \underbrace{s_t + \mathrm{div}(\mathrm{flux}(\dots))}_{\text{local, conserving}}
            \;+\; \underbrace{g_\theta^{\text{spectral}}(s_t)}_{\text{global, FNO-style}} .
\end{equation}
The global stream provides $O(1)$-layer global reach at the cost of $\sim$134k parameters.

\paragraph{(F) MultiChannelFluxNCA (per-field conservation).} For multi-field PDEs,
the flux head emits $2C$ channels---a flux $(f_x,f_y)$ per field---and each field's
mass is conserved by its own per-channel divergence. This is the
correct prior for periodic conservation laws (shallow-water mass and momentum) and a
deliberately inappropriate prior for source-term reaction systems (FitzHugh--Nagumo).

\paragraph{(G) Fourier Neural Operator (FNO).} Each layer mixes a pointwise linear map
with a truncated spectral convolution:
\begin{equation}
  v_{l+1} = \sigma\!\Big( W v_l + \mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(R_l \odot \mathcal{F}[v_l]\big)\Big),
\end{equation}
truncating to the lowest $m\times m$ Fourier modes. \textbf{Strength:} global,
multiscale mixing in few layers; resolution-invariant; native to periodic grids.
\textbf{Weakness:} spectral truncation smooths sharp features, and the spectral weights
are large ($\sim$$5.9\times10^5$ params here, versus the NCAs' $\sim$$5\times10^3$).

\paragraph{(H) PINN.} A continuous network $u_\phi(x,t)$ minimizing a residual loss via
autodiff:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \lambda_r\big\|\partial_t u_\phi - \mathcal{N}[u_\phi]\big\|^2
              + \lambda_b\big\|\mathcal{B}[u_\phi]\big\|^2
              + \lambda_0\big\|u_\phi(\cdot,0)-u_0\big\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Mesh-free, no labelled data, one IVP per training run. \textbf{Weakness:} spectral bias,
loss-balancing pathologies, no cross-IC reuse.

\paragraph{(I) DeepONet.} A branch network encodes the input function (at sensors) and a
trunk network encodes the query point; the output is their inner product,
$G(a)(y)\approx\sum_k b_k(a)\,t_k(y)$. It is an operator (cross-IC) trained on solver
pairs.

experiments isolate.

\subsection{Training protocol and the pre-seeding recipe}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every emulator is trained identically: same teacher, same initial conditions, same
horizon, same optimizer (AdamW), and the same rollout-MSE loss. The \emph{only}
difference between runs is the architecture. The shared pipeline is: draw a random IC;
\emph{pre-seed} it by evolving it with the solver for \texttt{preseed\_steps}
(producing a developed state); roll out both the teacher and the model over $K$ steps;
compute MSE; update with value-and-grad through the whole trajectory (BPTT via
\texttt{lax.scan}); and evaluate on a longer horizon from held-out ICs.

The original implementation used a specific recipe that is reproduced here:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single fixed seed (42)} --- the originals used \texttt{manual\_seed(42)}
        with no multi-seed averaging.
  \item \textbf{He / Kaiming-normal init} for ReLU-preceding convolutions.
  \item \textbf{Zero-init flux head} (identity start).
  \item \textbf{LR warmup} (linear$\to$constant schedule).
  \item \textbf{Pre-seeding} --- pre-evolve the IC into a developed state so the model
        trains on the developed-pattern regime rather than early diffusion.
\end{itemize}
A controlled comparison (heat, \texttt{multiscale\_flux\_nca}, seed 42, 150 epochs)
confirms that pre-seeding is the primary source of improvement: accuracy improves
($0.0328\to0.0270$) and, more importantly, long-horizon stability is substantially
enhanced (error-growth ratio $2.54\to1.41$). He-init and warmup are marginal in this
setting but are retained for fidelity. This protocol improved most PDEs relative to
the prior multi-seed numbers (heat spectral $0.016\to0.0064$, SWE
\texttt{mc\_flux}$\to0.016$, NS \texttt{fno} $0.145\to0.098$, wave $0.108\to0.052$)
but \emph{degraded} Cahn--Hilliard---pre-seeding mismatches its fresh-IC
coarsening---so CH uses \texttt{preseed\_steps=0}. The \emph{orderings} are stable;
only the precise numerical values shift. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} sketches the shared
pipeline.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Task.} One-step autoregressive emulation of a PDE solver teacher. Each model
is trained to match the teacher's \texttt{rollout\_steps}-step rollout from random ICs,
then evaluated on a longer \texttt{eval\_steps} horizon from held-out ICs (a different
PRNG stream)---testing temporal generalization. The reduced-scale CPU presets are
typically grid $24$ (2-D) or $16^3$ (3-D), train $12$ / eval $48$ steps, 150--200
epochs.

\paragraph{Metrics.} A full 20-metric panel is computed for every phenomenon:
rel-L2 (primary accuracy), MSE, RMSE, MAE, $L^\infty$, PSNR, SSIM, a
\emph{high-frequency error fraction} (the proportion of error residing in fine scales),
an \emph{error-growth profile} (rel-L2 at $T/4, T/2, 3T/4, T$ and the growth ratio),
mass-conservation error ($|\mathrm{mass}(\mathrm{pred})-\mathrm{mass}(\mathrm{IC})|$),
periodic-BC residual, gradient-energy (a stability proxy), parameter count, training
wall-clock, inference seconds/step, and throughput (cells/s).
Table~\ref{tab:heat20} gives the full panel for heat to make the protocol concrete.

\paragraph{Reduced-scale CPU.} The host is a single CPU (no GPU; Python 3.14.5,
jax 0.10.1 CPU build). Grids, horizons, epochs, and seed counts are small. Absolute
numbers are therefore not GPU-scale, and \texttt{pmap}/sharding paths are no-ops on a
single device. The \emph{relative} architecture orderings are the object of study and
are expected to transfer; the only difference between CPU and GPU presets is numeric
configuration fields, so the identical code re-runs at full scale.

\paragraph{Single fixed seed.} Headline numbers use seed 42 with the better-start
protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); a \texttt{--seeds N} variance study is retained
and multi-seed mean$\pm$std is reported where available (the per-PDE tables below).

\subsection{Smooth local diffusion (heat): the conservative NCAs attain the lowest error}
On heat, the local/multi-scale conservative NCAs prevail. Under the better-start
protocol the \texttt{spectral\_flux\_nca} reaches rel-L2 $0.0064$, the
\texttt{multiscale} reaches $0.027$, and even the plain conservative \texttt{pi\_nca}
($0.031$) edges the FNO ($0.021$ better-start; $0.0352$ in the multi-seed run). The
mechanism is direct: diffusion \emph{is} a local stencil, so a local conservative
machine carries exactly the appropriate bias. In the multi-seed run
(Table~\ref{tab:heatms}), the \texttt{multiscale} hybrid achieves the best result at
$0.0183\pm0.0016$, surpassing the FNO ($0.0352$) and the plain conservative PI-NCA
($0.0438$) at $\sim$110$\times$ fewer parameters, with the plain unconstrained NCA
diverging ($0.234$). Conservation is the decisive factor for NCA viability in this
regime: the unconstrained NCA's mass error is $33$, whereas the flux-divergence form
conserves to $3.8\times10^{-4}$---five orders of magnitude smaller, by construction.

\subsection{Globally coupled flow (Navier--Stokes): the FNO attains the lowest error}
The complement of the heat regime. Incompressible 2-D vorticity has a \emph{global}
Poisson coupling ($\nabla^2\psi=-\omega$): the velocity at every point depends on the
entire vorticity field. A local NCA cannot propagate information rapidly enough, and
the FNO attains the lowest error by a wide margin (rel-L2 $0.145$ multi-seed; $0.098$ better-start) versus the
best NCA ($0.284$, multi-scale). Among NCAs the \emph{multi-scale} variant performs
best because its dilated perception partially captures the non-locality; the
single-stencil plain ($0.528$) and conservative \texttt{pi\_nca} ($0.676$) lag
considerably. The PDE's information-propagation structure thus governs the choice of
architecture: locality with multi-scale perception prevails on purely local diffusion,
whereas global spectral mixing prevails on globally coupled flow.

\subsection{Fine-scale sharp interfaces (Allen--Cahn): the FNO attains the lowest error}
Allen--Cahn develops sharp interfaces, i.e.\ fine-scale content. The FNO attains the
lowest error ($0.0068$), with the NCAs around $0.049$. This connects to the spectral
diagnostic below:
the NCAs over-smooth, incurring the largest penalty precisely where the solution is
sharpest.

\subsection{Non-conservative reaction (Nagumo, FHN): the unconstrained NCA attains the lowest error}
On Nagumo and FitzHugh--Nagumo the reaction \emph{source term} modifies total mass, so
the conservation prior is structurally inappropriate. The unconstrained \texttt{plain\_nca}
attains the lowest error (Nagumo $0.073$/$0.118$ multi-seed; FHN $0.125$/$0.154$
multi-seed), while the conserving models stall (Nagumo $0.379$; FHN \texttt{mc\_flux}
$0.993$, which conserves total $u,v$ exactly at $1.1\times10^{-6}$ while being
practically useless). The conservation prior improves accuracy only when the governing
PDE is conservative.

\subsection{Conservative multi-field systems (shallow-water): per-field conservation is the appropriate prior}
On periodic shallow-water, mass \emph{and} momentum are conserved, so the per-channel
flux prior is correct. The \texttt{mc\_flux\_nca} matches the FNO on accuracy
($0.031$ vs $0.026$) at $54\times$ fewer parameters, with conservation four orders of
magnitude better ($1.6\times10^{-4}$ vs $0.77$). The FNO achieves marginally higher
accuracy, but at 593k parameters.

\subsection{Stiff fourth-order dynamics (Cahn--Hilliard): all baselines diverge until output bounding is added}
Every simple emulator diverges in this regime: against an identity (do-nothing) floor
of rel-L2 $\approx 0.93$, the baselines score $14$--$18$; they do not merely underfit
but diverge over the $48$-step horizon ($4\times$ the $12$-step training horizon). The
FNO's global mixing does not prevent failure (it exhibits large seed variance,
$15\pm15$), contrary to the expectation that a global operator is uniformly preferable.
The conservative \texttt{pi\_nca} conserves mass to $3\times10^{-5}$ while recording the
worst accuracy---it conserves the wrong dynamics, an empirical demonstration that
conservation does not imply correctness. The remedy (ablation A1) is to bound the
output; the \texttt{bounded\_cons\_nca} hybrid then reaches $0.603$ (multi-seed) /
$0.725$ (preseed=0 better-start), below the floor, \emph{and} conserves mass to
$2.4\times10^{-4}$---the only model that is simultaneously accurate and conserving.

\subsection{Spectral diagnostic: where the NCAs' error concentrates}
\label{sec:spectral}
The high-frequency error fraction accounts for why the FNO prevails on sharp-interface
problems despite the NCAs' lower total error on smooth ones. On heat, the NCAs place
$0.44$--$0.89$ of their error in fine scales, whereas the FNO's error is broadband-flat
at $0.004$ (multi-seed; $0.068$ in the single-seed panel of Table~\ref{tab:heat20}).
The NCAs are accurate overall but over-smooth: their residual error concentrates in the
highest frequencies, while the FNO distributes its error evenly across the spectrum.
This accounts for the FNO's advantage on Allen--Cahn and other fine-scale,
sharp-interface regimes, whose solutions are dominated by exactly the fine scales the
NCAs suppress.

\subsection{Parameter efficiency on heat}
\label{sec:efficiency}
Accuracy alone understates the parameter efficiency of the NCAs. Measuring the
parameter cost of accuracy as rel-L2$\times$params (lower is better), Table~\ref{tab:eff}
places the hybrid multi-scale NCA at $101$ versus the standard FNO at $20{,}875$, a
$207\times$ gap. The hybrid is simultaneously more accurate ($0.0183$ vs $0.0352$) and
$107\times$ smaller (5.5k vs 593k params), and roughly $6\times$ cheaper at inference
(Table~\ref{tab:heat20}). The FNO achieves a global receptive field---essential on
globally-coupled PDEs---at a substantial parameter and compute premium.

\subsection{Resolution transfer}
\label{sec:resolution}

A recurring claim for the FNO is resolution invariance: a model trained on one grid
should evaluate accurately on another. We test this by training each model on a fixed
grid (train grid $\in\{16,24,32,48\}$) and evaluating on each grid in the same set,
reporting rel-L2 for every (train, eval) pair. Table~\ref{tab:restransfer} gives the
$4\times4$ transfer matrix on heat for the conv-based \texttt{multiscale\_flux\_nca}
and the FNO; the Allen--Cahn and Navier--Stokes results are summarized below.

The transfer behavior is regime-dependent, and on heat it does not match the
resolution-invariance expectation for either family in a simple way.

\paragraph{Heat.} The conv-based NCA transfers from coarse to fine grids well
(train $16\to$ eval $48$ gives $0.029$) but degrades from fine to coarse
(train $48\to$ eval $16$ gives $0.349$). The FNO shows the opposite of resolution
invariance in this autoregressive local-diffusion setting: its error is low only near
the training resolution and grows sharply away from it (train $16\to$ eval $48$ gives
$0.504$; train $48\to$ eval $16$ gives $0.782$), with the diagonal (same-resolution)
entries the lowest in each row. For reference, the plain \texttt{pi\_nca} follows the
same pattern as the multi-scale NCA (train $16\to$ eval $48$: $0.045$;
train $48\to$ eval $16$: $0.380$). The FNO's spectral parameterization is defined on a
fixed set of Fourier modes, so under autoregressive rollout of a purely local diffusion
operator it does not generalize across resolutions here. A further observation is that
the NCAs' same-resolution accuracy improves as the grid is refined
($0.076$ at $16^2 \to 0.008$ at $48^2$ for the multi-scale model), consistent with the
local stencil resolving the diffusion operator more finely.

\paragraph{Allen--Cahn.} Both families are resolution-robust. The
\texttt{multiscale\_flux\_nca} is roughly flat across all train/eval grids
($\approx 0.049$--$0.056$), and the FNO is robust and low
($\approx 0.012$--$0.026$, best near $0.012$--$0.016$; e.g.\ train $16$ gives $0.021$
at eval $16$ and $0.026$ at eval $48$).

\paragraph{Navier--Stokes.} The FNO is genuinely resolution-invariant, with rel-L2
stable in the range $\approx 0.183$--$0.237$ across all train/eval grid combinations
(train $48\to$ eval $16$ gives $0.202$). This is the setting the FNO is designed
for---a globally coupled operator---and its fixed-mode spectral parameterization
transfers cleanly. The \texttt{multiscale\_flux\_nca}, by contrast, fails to transfer
and diverges when trained at $48^2$ (eval $16$: $2.387$; eval $48$: $1.252$),
consistent with the locality limitation identified in the Navier--Stokes results above.

In summary, resolution invariance is not an intrinsic property of an architecture but a
property of the architecture-PDE pair: the FNO transfers across resolutions on the
globally coupled Navier--Stokes system but not under autoregressive local diffusion,
whereas the conv-NCAs transfer coarse-to-fine on heat but fail on Navier--Stokes; both
families are robust on Allen--Cahn.

% ============================================================
\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablation}
% ============================================================

The ablations isolate \emph{which component} drives performance, confirming that the
answer is regime-dependent. Table~\ref{tab:ablsummary} summarizes.

\paragraph{A1 --- Output bounding (Cahn--Hilliard).} Clipping each emulator step to the
field's physical range $[-1,1]$ (as the solver does) takes the NCAs from divergence to
below the identity floor: \texttt{plain\_nca} $12.93\to0.540$ and
\texttt{pi\_nca} $16.51\to0.600$---a $24$--$27\times$ improvement. The CH failure was a
\emph{stability/blow-up} problem, architecturally correctable, not an intrinsic limit
of local emulation. There is, however, a constraint: hard clipping \emph{destroys} the
flux-NCA's exact conservation ($3.3\times10^{-5}\to7.6$)---clip is a non-conservative
projection. The two desirable properties (boundedness, exact conservation) \emph{conflict},
which motivates the bounded conservation-preserving \texttt{bounded\_cons\_nca}. Once
clipping dominates, plain and conservative NCAs become statistically comparable ($0.54$
vs $0.60$): on stiff CH, \emph{bounding} is more consequential than \emph{conservation}
for accuracy---reversing the heat ranking.

\paragraph{A2 --- Iso-parameter FNO (heat).} Shrinking the FNO to the NCA budget
($8.4$k params) degrades it $3.4\times$ ($0.035\to0.119$), making it worse than both
NCA-budget models. The FNO's heat advantage therefore stems largely from parameter
count rather than from spectral mixing per se; the multi-scale NCA outperforms both the
full and the iso-parameter FNO at $\sim$1\% of the parameters.

\paragraph{A3 --- Horizon curriculum (Cahn--Hilliard).} Matching the training horizon to
the eval horizon ($12\to24\to48$) reduces rel-L2 by $0.633\to0.619\to0.511$
($\sim$19\% relative) and conservation error by $3.2\times10^{-4}\to6.8\times10^{-5}$.
The train/eval gap was a real, \emph{separate} contributor on top of bounding (A1).
The combined approach---bounded+conserving and train$\approx$eval---reaches CH $0.51$,
well below the floor, with near-exact conservation.

\paragraph{A4 --- Conservation on/off at matched width.} With the \emph{same} backbone
and only the head differing (flux/conserving vs residual/free), conservation
\emph{helps} heat ($0.104$ vs $0.353$, $3.4\times$) and \emph{hurts} Nagumo ($0.379$
vs $0.123$, $3.1\times$). This provides a clean, matched-width demonstration of the
central finding: the conservation head is beneficial when the PDE conserves mass and
detrimental when it does not.

\paragraph{A5 --- Perception / receptive-field size.} Widening perception (3$\times$3
vs 5$\times$5 vs dilated multi-scale 1/2/4) improves both regimes, but the method
matters: on heat all three improve with reach ($0.104\to0.046\to0.024$); on
globally-coupled Navier--Stokes, a plain 5$\times$5 stencil \emph{destabilizes}
($1.486$, worse than 3$\times$3's $0.576$) while dilated multi-scale is best ($0.388$).
Multi-scale dilation is thus the robust mechanism for widening the receptive field.

\paragraph{A6 --- Single- vs multi-step (BPTT) training.} Training through the
trajectory (rollout 1/4/12) is \emph{nearly irrelevant} on stable heat
($0.0250\to0.0210$, 16\%) but \emph{decisive} on unstable Navier--Stokes:
single-step-trained emulators diverge ($1.562$, error-growth $13.5\times$) while
12-step training stabilizes the long rollout ($0.284$, error-growth
$2.76\times$), a $5.5\times$ gain. The model must observe its own accumulated error
during training in order to control it, and this matters most where the dynamics are
most demanding.

\section{Three-Dimensional Generalization}
\label{sec:threed}
% ============================================================

To test whether the regime map is an artifact of two dimensions, six PDEs are
re-implemented in full 3-D (NDHWC, $16^3$ grid, 7-point Laplacian, 3-D flux divergence
over all three axes, 3-D spectral convolution via \texttt{rfftn}; 3-D correctness gate
11/11) and the comparison is repeated. Every 2-D conclusion reproduces
(Table~\ref{tab:regime3d}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local diffusion:} conservative NCA prevails (heat: \texttt{pi\_nca}
        $0.047$ at $\sim$3k params versus the FNO's 747k).
  \item \textbf{Sharp interfaces} $\to$ FNO (Allen--Cahn $0.012$).
  \item \textbf{Non-conservative reaction} $\to$ FNO or unconstrained NCA; the
        conservation prior \emph{hurts} non-conservative Nagumo (conserving NCAs $0.198$
        vs plain $0.041$); on FitzHugh--Nagumo \texttt{mc\_flux} conserves exactly but
        is practically useless ($0.99$).
  \item \textbf{Conservation behavior:} the NCAs conserve mass to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-6}$
        while plain/FNO drift by $10$--$600$.
\end{itemize}
The dependence of architecture performance on PDE structure is therefore
dimension-independent. Figure~\ref{fig:heat3d} shows the heat 3-D volume rendering and a
mid-depth slice montage.

\section{Continuous and Operator Baselines}
\label{sec:operators}
% ============================================================

The PINN and DeepONet address a \emph{different task} from the emulators---a single IVP
(PINN) or a fixed-horizon operator (DeepONet)---and are scored on a different horizon.
They are reported as within-paradigm baselines, \emph{not} a head-to-head accuracy race
(Table~\ref{tab:operators}).

On heat, DeepONet ($0.075$) clearly surpasses the PINN ($0.208$): it is an operator
trained on solver data that generalizes across initial conditions, whereas the vanilla
PINN solves one IVP from the equation alone and incurs the spectral-bias/loss-balancing
penalty. The trade-off is clear: the PINN requires \emph{zero training data} and is
mesh-free and continuously queryable; DeepONet requires solver pairs but achieves
substantially higher accuracy and trains in seconds. Neither matches the best
autoregressive emulator's rollout accuracy on heat, but the metrics are not
commensurable.

\paragraph{Darcy flow (steady elliptic operator $a\mapsto u$).} On a high-contrast
piecewise coefficient Darcy problem (grid 20, 256 train samples), both models are
mediocre, but the \textbf{learned-iterative NCA solver edges the FNO at $144\times$
fewer parameters} ($0.535$, 4.1k params vs $0.594$, 593k params). Relaxing $u$ from
zero over 24 local steps is a natural fit for an elliptic solve (akin to a learned
Jacobi/multigrid smoother), and over a 20-grid, 24 steps nearly suffices to propagate
boundary information. The FNO trains $5\times$ faster (no inner rollout). The
interpretation is that \emph{locality is not disqualifying on a static elliptic problem
the way it is on dynamic Navier--Stokes}.

\section{CAX Accelerator Evaluation}
\label{sec:cax}
% ============================================================

We evaluated whether the CAX library (Cellular Automata Accelerated in JAX,
\texttt{arXiv:2410.02651}) could accelerate our rollouts. The key observation is that
CAX's rollout driver is \texttt{nnx.scan} + \texttt{nnx.jit}---\emph{structurally
identical} to our hand-written \texttt{jax.lax.scan} rollout; both compile to the same
XLA scan. There is no separate kernel or hardware path that would make CAX intrinsically
faster on the same device. A measurement (single CPU, grid 32, 64 steps, batch 8) gives
our \texttt{lax.scan} \texttt{DeepFluxNCA} ($C=1$) at $\sim$1.17 ms/step versus a CAX
\texttt{ComplexSystem} NCA ($C=16$ + hidden) at $\sim$2.42 ms/step---though this is not
parameter-matched (the CAX NCA performs more work). \textbf{CAX provides no single-CPU
rollout speedup}; its value lies in its CA zoo (Lenia, Flow-Lenia), its ready-made
Moore/von-Neumann perception stencils (useful as A5 neighbourhood variants), and
multi-device scaling when hardware is available. It was therefore not integrated into
the hot path, but a working CAX NCA subclass is retained as a reference integration
point.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

The results support the central finding: no single architecture attains the lowest
error across all PDEs, and the PDE's structure---conservation, boundedness,
information-propagation range, and fine-scale content---determines the appropriate
architecture.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Conservation} is beneficial if and only if the PDE is conservative. It
        makes the local NCA viable on heat (the unconstrained NCA diverges) and allows a
        compact per-field conservative NCA to match the FNO on shallow-water; it actively
        \emph{degrades} non-conservative reaction systems (Nagumo, FHN), where it
        enforces the wrong totals. Ablation A4 demonstrates this at matched width.
  \item \textbf{Information-propagation range} governs the locality-versus-global
        choice. Heat (purely local) and Navier--Stokes (global Poisson) are mirror
        images: locality with multi-scale perception prevails on the former, global spectral mixing on the latter.
  \item \textbf{Fine-scale content} accounts for the spectral diagnostic: NCAs
        over-smooth (high-frequency error fraction $0.44$--$0.89$) while the FNO is
        broadband-flat ($0.004$), so the FNO prevails on sharp-interface problems
        (Allen--Cahn) despite the NCA's lower total error on smooth ones.
  \item \textbf{Boundedness / stiffness} constitutes its own axis. On Cahn--Hilliard
        the failure mode is blow-up, not locality, and the remedy is output
        bounding---which then conflicts with conservation and necessitates a bounded
        conservation-preserving design.
\end{itemize}

The hybrid designs attain the lowest error in their target regimes. A model that
composes the corresponding biases outperforms every pure baseline in its regime: the
multi-scale conservative NCA on smooth local diffusion (more accurate than the FNO at
$\sim$1\% of its parameters), and the bounded conservation-preserving NCA on stiff
Cahn--Hilliard (the only tested model both accurate and conserving). Composing all
components does not automatically improve performance: multi-scale perception helps
smooth transport but not stiff-CH accuracy, where bounding is decisive. A single unified
\texttt{MultiScaleFluxNCA} with PDE-appropriate bounds is best or near-best in both
regimes (\texttt{bounds=None} is best on heat; \texttt{bounds=(-1,1)} matches the CH winner
at $\sim$6$\times$ lower variance). The conservative NCA's flux-divergence structure is
the primary reusable component---compact, exactly mass-conserving, and composable with
wider perception or bounding as the PDE demands.

For PDE-governed systems, the comparison of NCA versus PINN versus neural operator is
not the appropriate framing. The appropriate framing is \emph{which inductive bias
matches the PDE's structural characteristics}: locality + conservation for smooth local
transport; global spectral mixing for cross-IC generalization on periodic grids;
explicit boundedness/stabilization for stiff dynamics; and continuous mesh-free PINNs
for single-IVP or irregular-query problems.

% ============================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reduced-scale CPU.} Small grids, horizons, epochs, and seed counts on a
        single CPU (no GPU). Absolute numbers are not GPU-scale;
        \texttt{pmap}/sharding paths are no-ops on one device. The relative orderings
        are the object of study, and identical configs re-run at full scale by modifying
        only numeric configuration fields.
  \item \textbf{Single-seed trade-off.} Headline numbers use a single fixed seed (42)
        per the original implementation. This is deterministic and reproducible, but a
        single seed may be statistically favorable or unfavorable (e.g.\ multiscale on
        heat was $0.021$ as the mean of seeds 0,1 vs $0.033$ at seed 42). A
        \texttt{--seeds N} variance study is retained, and multi-seed mean$\pm$std is
        reported where available; init-robust signals (error-growth, spectral
        diagnostics) are used where a single number could be misleading.
  \item \textbf{Protocol caveat for Cahn--Hilliard.} Pre-seeding improves most PDEs but
        mismatches CH's fresh-IC coarsening, so CH uses \texttt{preseed\_steps=0}.
  \item \textbf{PINN/DeepONet are heat-only} and use different horizons/metrics than the
        emulators; they are a separate paradigm, not a direct comparison.
  \item \textbf{Some sweeps remain.} A number of emulator sweeps are scaffolded but not
        exhaustively run; all are reproducible from \texttt{bench.py}.
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

This study examined whether a local PI-NCA can surpass PINNs and neural operators on
PDEs, finding that performance is strictly problem-dependent: no architecture dominates
universally. Across ten 2-D and six 3-D phenomena under one shared protocol, the
PDE's structure---conservation, boundedness, information-propagation range, and
fine-scale content---selects the architecture. Hybrid architectures that compose the
appropriate inductive biases surpass every pure baseline in their respective regimes:
a multi-scale conservative NCA on smooth local transport (more accurate than the FNO
at $\sim$200$\times$ better parameter efficiency) and a bounded conservation-preserving
NCA on stiff dynamics (the only model both accurate and mass-conserving). This finding
is dimension-independent. The practical implication for practitioners is a structured
decision framework rather than a universally recommended method: identify the PDE's
structural characteristics, and select (or compose) the inductive bias that corresponds
to them.

\section{Reproducibility}

All results are reproducible from the project repository (branch
\texttt{research/jax-migration}). The verified stack is jax 0.10.1 (CPU), flax 0.12.7,
optax 0.2.8, cax 0.3.3, torch 2.12.0 (CPU, reference only), Python 3.14.5 on Windows 11

deterministic across thread counts).

# Correctness gate (run first; must be green) -- 56/56 total
python -m pytest tests/ -q                         # 2-D migration/suite/metrics (36)

python -m pinca_jax.bench --pde cahn_hilliard --seeds 3 --epochs 200 --grid 24

# Reproduce the 3-D benchmarks
python -m pinca_jax.bench3d --grid 16 --epochs 120

# Continuous baselines and CAX evaluation
python -m pinca_jax.pinn_heat
python -m pinca_jax.cax_eval
